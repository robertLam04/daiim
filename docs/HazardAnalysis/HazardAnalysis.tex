\section{Introduction}

\section{Introduction}

A hazard is a condition or event that could cause harm or loss. For
\progname{}, this could include an inaccurate inference, exposure of sensitive
input data or wallet keys, or mishandling a transaction involving users' funds.
\\\\
Hazard analysis is especially important for \progname{} because errors cannot be
easily corrected after the fact. Blockchain transactions are irreversible, so
there is no central party that can intervene to reverse a harmful outcome.
Identifying potential hazards early allows the team to address them while the design
is still flexible.

\section{Scope and Purpose of Hazard Analysis}

The purpose of the hazard analysis is to identify potential hazards within the DAIIM system, examine their causes and effects, assess their associated risks, and establish mitigation strategies to eliminate or reduce the likelihood and severity of these hazards. The analysis considers the system components and boundaries defined in Section 2, with particular emphasis on the reliability of AI inference results, the integrity and security of marketplace operations, and the fairness of participant incentives.
The hazard analysis will focus on identifying hazards that may lead to the following:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Inference accuracy:} Incorrect, inconsistent, or unreliable aggregated predictions resulting from inaccurate model outputs, weaknesses in the aggregation algorithm, or insufficiently reliable inference providers.
\item \textbf{Data and transaction integrity:} Loss, corruption, or inconsistency of inference requests, prediction results, reputation information, or transaction records, potentially resulting in incorrect outcomes or payments.

\item \textbf{Security and malicious participation:} Unauthorized actions or deliberate attempts to manipulate the marketplace, including collusion between inference providers to influence aggregated predictions, reputation scores, or reward distribution.

\item \textbf{System availability and reliability:} Failure to submit, process, or return inference requests due to unavailable providers, failed transactions, or disruptions affecting the application's operation.

\item \textbf{Economic incentives and fairness:} Incorrect payment calculations, reward distributions, or stake handling that result in financial losses for task requesters or inference providers. The analysis also considers weaknesses in the reward and penalty mechanisms that fail to incentivize accurate predictions, allow malicious or colluding providers to benefit unfairly, or undermine the reliability and economic sustainability of the marketplace.

\end{itemize}
The analysis will consider hazards arising from the interactions between the application's components, including the off-chain backend, AI inference providers, aggregation and reputation mechanisms, and on-chain programs. External dependencies, such as blockchain network infrastructure and third-party inference services, will be considered where their failures or limitations could affect the safety, security, reliability, or intended operation of DAIIM. Hardware failures and other external events beyond the system's boundaries will otherwise remain outside the scope of the analysis.

\section{System Boundaries and Components}

This section divides DAIIM into its major components and identifies where the
boundary of the system lies. Components inside the boundary are built,
deployed, and maintained by the team, and their behaviour can be changed to
mitigate hazards. Components outside the boundary are part of DAIIM's
environment: DAIIM depends on them, but cannot control them, so hazards
originating from them can only be detected, tolerated, or contained. The
components below are based on the requirements in the SRS and the technologies
listed in the Development Plan. They are preliminary and will be refined in the
design documents.

\subsection{Components Within the System Boundary}

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Web Client.} The browser-based user interface, built with React,
through which task requesters and inference providers use DAIIM. It connects to
the user's wallet for authentication, lets users browse and create tasks,
submit inference requests, register as providers, and view results,
reputations, stakes, and rewards. Before each transaction, it shows the user
the action being performed and the amount of tokens being transferred, and
afterwards it shows the transaction's status and identifier. It also performs
an initial check that request inputs conform to the task's input schema.
\textit{Related SRS requirements:} Accounts and Authorization, Inference Tasks,
Inference Requests, Aggregation and Results, Transparency.

\item \textbf{On-Chain Programs.} The Solana programs, written in Rust, that
hold DAIIM's authoritative state and all of its tokens. They are responsible
for:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the registry of inference tasks and their parameters (minimum
  providers, reputation tolerance, acceptance period, maximum prediction time);
  \item provider registration and the escrow of provider stakes;
  \item escrow of requester payments and the lifecycle of each inference
  request (acceptance period, prediction phase, cancellation and refunds);
  \item recording predictions, aggregating them into the final result, and
  updating provider reputations;
  \item distributing rewards, and slashing or burning provider stakes.
\end{itemize}
Because this component holds funds and blockchain transactions cannot be
reversed, a defect here can cause direct and permanent financial loss.
\textit{Related SRS requirements:} Inference Tasks, Provider Registration and
Staking, Provider Acceptance and Predictions, Aggregation and Results,
Reputation and Rewards, Penalties, Transparency.

\item \textbf{Off-Chain Backend Service.} A Node.js and TypeScript service that
performs the work that is impractical or unsafe to do on-chain. It monitors the
on-chain programs for new inference requests and broadcasts them to the
providers registered for the task, stores request input data and distributes it
only to the requester and the providers that accepted the request, and deletes
the input data once the request is completed or cancelled. It may also submit
the transactions that move a request from one phase to the next when a deadline
passes, since on-chain programs cannot act on their own, and it serves
marketplace information to the Web Client. Since this service is a single
point that the team operates, it must not be able to change results, payments,
or reputations, which are controlled only by the on-chain programs.
\textit{Related SRS requirements:} Inference Requests, Provider Acceptance and
Predictions, Data Handling.

\item \textbf{Off-Chain Database.} A PostgreSQL database used by the backend
service. It temporarily holds encrypted request input data, which must not be
stored on the blockchain, as well as task information that is too large to
store on-chain economically (such as problem descriptions, schemas, and
references to training data) and indexed copies of on-chain records for fast
browsing. The on-chain records remain the source of truth for any data that
appears in both places. \textit{Related SRS requirements:} Inference Tasks,
Data Handling.

\item \textbf{Provider Interface.} The interface, such as an API or client
library, through which an inference provider's model receives broadcast
requests, accepts requests, retrieves input data, and submits predictions. It
checks that predictions conform to the task's output schema before they are
submitted. This component ends where the provider's own model begins.
\textit{Related SRS requirements:} Provider Acceptance and Predictions.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Components Outside the System Boundary}

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Solana Blockchain Network.} The public Solana network, including
its validators and the RPC nodes used to read state and submit transactions.
DAIIM relies on it to execute the on-chain programs, store records, and provide
the time used for request deadlines. It may become congested, raise fees, delay
or drop transactions, or suffer outages.

\item \textbf{User Wallets.} Third-party Web3 wallets, such as browser wallet
extensions, that hold users' private keys and sign transactions. DAIIM cannot
recover a lost key, and cannot distinguish a compromised wallet from its
legitimate owner.

\item \textbf{Inference Providers' Models and Infrastructure.} The machine
learning models, computing resources, and network connections of each
provider. DAIIM receives only the predictions they produce and cannot inspect
the models. Providers may be unavailable, inaccurate, careless, or dishonest,
and several providers may collude.

\item \textbf{Task Requesters and Their Input Data.} The people who define tasks
and submit requests, and the sources of the input data they provide. DAIIM can
check that inputs conform to a task's schema, but cannot verify that the data
is correct, that task parameters are sensible, or that the data is not
sensitive.

\item \textbf{Downstream Decisions.} The decisions that task requesters and
beneficiaries, such as emergency management agencies, make using DAIIM's
results. DAIIM is responsible for providing inferences and information
about their quality, but not for how the results are used.

\item \textbf{Hosting Infrastructure and Browsers.} The cloud or server
infrastructure that hosts the backend service and database, and the web
browsers on which the Web Client runs.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Critical Assumptions}

The following assumptions describe the conditions under which the DAIIM system is expected to operate. These assumptions establish the operating context of the system and identify external conditions on which its functionality depends. Potential failures and malicious behaviour within the system, such as inaccurate predictions, provider collusion, and manipulation of reward distribution, are not assumed to be impossible and will instead be addressed through the hazard analysis.
\begin{itemize}
\item The underlying blockchain network is operational and capable of processing transactions under normal operating conditions.
\begin{itemize}
\item This assumption establishes the expected operating environment for on-chain marketplace operations. Temporary network outages, congestion, and failed transactions remain potential hazards that must be considered.
\end{itemize}
\item Inference providers have access to the computational resources and model implementations required to execute the inference tasks they accept.
\begin{itemize}
\item This assumption defines the expected capabilities of participating providers. Provider unavailability, insufficient computational resources, and failed inference requests remain potential hazards.
\end{itemize}

\item A minimum number of two inference providers is available during the initial operation of the marketplace.
\begin{itemize}
    \item This assumption establishes that the marketplace has some initial provider participation to process inference requests and evaluate the aggregation, reputation, and reward mechanisms. 
\end{itemize}

\item Users have access to a modern web browser and an internet connection to interact with the application.
\begin{itemize}
    \item This assumption defines the minimum environment required to access the user interface. Network interruptions and browser compatibility issues remain potential causes of service disruption.
\end{itemize}

\end{itemize}

\section{Safety and Security Requirements}

\wss{Newly discovered requirements.  These should also be added to the SRS.  (A
rationale design process how and why to fake it.)}

Based on the hazard analysis above, the following safety and security requirements have been outlined. Each requirement addresses a hazard in the FMEA table.

\subsection{Functional Requirements}

\noindent \begin{itemize}

\item[\refstepcounter{sanum}\thesanum \label{SA_NoDuplicateSubmission}:]
The system shall not resubmit a transaction until it has confirmed that the original transaction was not processed and shall maintain logs of all transaction statuses and log verified transactions publicly on-chain.

\item[\refstepcounter{sanum}\thesanum \label{SA_BalanceCheck}:]
The system shall verify if the wallet of a user has sufficient funds before requesting a signature and logging the transaction.

\item[\refstepcounter{sanum}\thesanum \label{SA_OnChainFundsCheck}:]
The on-chain programs shall reject any transaction that the wallet cannot cover at the time of execution, regardless of any balance check made earlier.

\item[\refstepcounter{sanum}\thesanum \label{SA_SignedTransactionsOnly}:]
The system shall not submit any transaction which is not signed by an authorized wallet on-chain or off-chain.

\item[\refstepcounter{sanum}\thesanum \label{SA_ExpiryCheck}:]
The system shall wait for validity period to end and status to be checked before logging transaction as failed and attempting to reprocess.

\item[\refstepcounter{sanum}\thesanum \label{SA_FailureRetry}:]
When a transaction fails, the system shall send the error to the user and allow them to retry.

\item[\refstepcounter{sanum}\thesanum \label{SA_TransactionSimulation}:]
Before a transaction is submitted for signing, the system shall simulate it and verify that the resulting balance changes equal the amounts displayed to the user, and shall not submit it otherwise.

\item[\refstepcounter{sanum}\thesanum \label{SA_ChargeEqualsAuthorized}:]
The amount charged to a requester for an inference request shall equal the payment amount the requester authorized.

\item[\refstepcounter{sanum}\thesanum \label{SA_RewardValidation}:]
The system shall validate each reward calculation before distributing it, and shall not distribute rewards whose total exceeds the reward for the request.

\item[\refstepcounter{sanum}\thesanum \label{SA_ProviderStatusCheck}:]
The system shall confirm provider status before distributing rewards/slashing tokens.

\item[\refstepcounter{sanum}\thesanum \label{SA_GroundTruthTests}:]
Providers shall be given random tests with a known ground truth to identify malicious providers.

\item[\refstepcounter{sanum}\thesanum \label{SA_RandomInvitations}:]
The system shall send invitation to accept an inference request to providers randomly, to reduce the possibility of premeditated collusion attacks.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Non-Functional Requirements}

\noindent \begin{itemize}

\item[\refstepcounter{snfrnum}\thesnfrnum \label{SA_ErrorMessageTime}:] The system shall send readable error messages to users within 2 seconds of a detected failure.

\item[\refstepcounter{snfrnum}\thesnfrnum \label{SA_WalletSigning}:] All signing shall happen within the user's wallet and private information shall not be logged on-chain.

\item[\refstepcounter{snfrnum}\thesnfrnum \label{SA_TLS}:] All communication between DAIIM components, and between users and DAIIM, shall use TLS with certificate validation enabled to ensure data protection.

\item[\refstepcounter{snfrnum}\thesnfrnum \label{SA_DependencyScan}:] The system shall scan third-party dependencies for known security vulnerabilities, verified through testing.

\end{itemize}


Other mitigation strategies include the implementation of simulations to gather data and justify protocols for mitigating malicous users and collusion attacks.


\section{Roadmap}

\wss{Which safety requirements will be implemented as part of the capstone timeline?
Which requirements will be implemented in the future?}


\subsection{Requirements Planned During Capstone}

The requirements that can be implemented within the timeline of the project are listed below, along with justification.

\begin{itemize}

\item \ref{SA_NoDuplicateSubmission}: Duplicate transactions are the most direct way users could lose funds. Checking a transaction's status before retrying is a small amount of logic, and we will need it as soon as we start testing transactions on Devnet. Transaction statuses are also needed to show progress to users and to debug the on-chain programs, and confirmed transactions are already recorded on-chain as part of the core design.

\item \ref{SA_BalanceCheck}: Checking balances before asking for a signature avoids failed transactions that could have been prevented. It is a simple lookup and is needed for the transaction preview anyway.

\item \ref{SA_OnChainFundsCheck}: Enforcing this in the on-chain programs protects funds no matter what the client checks, so it belongs with the development of the programs themselves.

\item \ref{SA_SignedTransactionsOnly}: The blockchain rejects unsigned transactions, so making sure our client never submits one is part of basic transaction handling.

\item \ref{SA_ExpiryCheck}: Transactions will expire during network congestion on Devnet. Retry handling needs to be built early so that expired transactions are not submitted twice.

\item \ref{SA_FailureRetry}: Users must be told when a transaction fails and be able to retry. The same error handling will also be what we rely on for debugging during development.

\item \ref{SA_TransactionSimulation}: Wrong payment amounts are likely to appear while we develop the payment instructions. Simulating transactions before signing will catch them before users are asked to authorize anything.

\item \ref{SA_ChargeEqualsAuthorized}: Charging the amount the requester authorized is the core of financial correctness, and a mistake here causes direct losses. It is part of the payment logic in the on-chain programs.

\item \ref{SA_RewardValidation}: A total-reward check is a simple invariant in the reward logic, and it catches calculation errors before tokens move.

\item \ref{SA_ProviderStatusCheck}: Paying or slashing a provider who is no longer registered would break the stake rules. Checking the provider's status first is a small addition to the staking logic.

\item \ref{SA_ErrorMessageTime}: Readable errors matter for both usability and debugging. The time limit is measured inside our own client, so we can test it on Devnet.

\item \ref{SA_WalletSigning}: Signing happens in the user's wallet by design (the embedded wallet for requesters, the provider's own signer through the SDK), so this requirement needs very little extra development.

\item \ref{SA_TLS}: TLS with certificate validation is a hosting and library configuration, and it is needed for any deployment, including demos.

\item \ref{SA_DependencyScan}: The use of the continuous integration pipeline to scan third-party dependencies will aid in identifying vulnerabilities promptly and is a natural addition when incorporating third party dependencies into the system. Once implemented, the scans will occur automatically.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Delayed Requirements}

The requirements that will be implemented in the future and the justification are detailed below.

\begin{itemize}

\item \ref{SA_GroundTruthTests}: Giving providers random tests with known answers needs tasks with known ground truth, test requests that cannot be told apart from real ones (otherwise providers would only behave well on tests), and a way to pay providers for requests that have no requester. This is an additional feature that would enhance security and improve provider integrity, but is beyond the scope of the project and is not essential to the main functionality.

\item \ref{SA_RandomInvitations}: Randomly inviting providers requires a secure, verifiable source of randomness on Solana, and conflicts with the current design in which every registered provider receives each request. It would also require reworking how the minimum number of providers is met. 

\end{itemize}

marked as preliminary and will be confirmed in the design documents. The team
remains responsible for the accuracy, completeness, and consistency of this
document, regardless of whether material was first drafted by a team member or

    the concretization of the project that came out of defining functional requirements.
    Up until this point, the problem statement, goals and development plan gave us a good definition
    of the scope and the reason for the project, but there have been several looming questions about
    what actions the system will perform. I think we were able to successfully define the core functional requirements
    for the system, at a good level of abstraction. Our requirements are specific enough to say a lot about what the
    system is responsible for, while not proposing possible designs. This process allowed us to answer a lot of the questions
    we had discovered during earlier deliverables. Although these requirements are likely to evolve
    as the project continues (especially during the POC), this felt like a significant milestone and marker of progress.
    \item What pain points did you experience during this deliverable, and how
    did you resolve them?
    A pain point we experienced for this deliverable is the organizational overhead that arose because of the volume
    of written content required. Because the SRS document and Hazard Analysis are large documents, it was often not
    straightforward to allocate sections for parallel development, review each other's work, and keep track of the work. We addressed these problems by assigning responsibility for each section, providing constructive feedback in GitHub pull requests, and
    reviewing the integrated documents together before final submission. This made it easier to work in parallel while catching gaps, overlap, and
    inconsistencies during review. It also made it a lot easier for each member to keep track of the work they were responsible for. We also plan to treat the documents as evolving artifacts, revisiting and refining earlier sections
    when later decisions clarified the system's requirements and hazards. We also established a standard of creating a GitHub issue
    for every major piece of work and tracking its progress on the Kanban board.
    \item Which of your listed risks had your team thought of before this
    deliverable, and which did you think of while doing this deliverable? For
    the latter ones (ones you thought of while doing the Hazard Analysis), how
    did they come about?
    We had already considered inaccurate or unreliable predictions, provider
    unavailability, and malicious participation in our dev plan (POC plan).
    These are the main risks that the POC aims to prove are avoidable.
    The Hazard Analysis brought more risks into focus relating to the software environment,
    including failed or expired transactions,
    private-key exposure through software or dependencies, and incorrect reward
    or stake handling. These emerged after defining the main functional requirements
    and considering how each responsibility could fail.
    \item Other than the risk of physical harm (some projects may not have any
    appreciable risks of this form), list at least 2 other types of risk in
    software products. Why are they important to consider?
    We talked about two other types of risk other than physical harm in the hazard analysis.
    Privacy risks arise if sensitive
    information is exposed to unauthorized parties. This can harm users and
    undermine their trust in the system. The other types of risk are legal and financial risks.
    Legal and financial risks can arise
    when transactions fail, are duplicated, or charge an incorrect amount.
    Users could lose funds or face disputes, and the project could incur
    liability. These risks are important
    to consider because they directly affect our stakeholders and, if we cannot
    mitigate them, could threaten the project's success.
