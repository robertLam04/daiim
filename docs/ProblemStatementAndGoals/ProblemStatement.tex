\section{Problem Statement}

\subsection{Problem}

Current AI inference markets are increasingly concentrated around a small number of large organizations~\cite{vipra2023market}.
As the artificial intelligence (AI) industry continues to grow, we become increasingly reliant on centralized providers for AI models, data, hardware, and compute.
This centralization increases the risk of biased outputs because of the lack of diverse data and training algorithms used by these companies~\cite{rolf2021representation}.
Furthermore, the oligopolistic nature of the inference market creates a risk of censorship and manipulation of model outputs. The lack of transparency from these companies and the black-box nature of many AI models make it difficult for consumers to verify the quality and reliability of the inferences they receive.
Lastly, the centralized nature of the compute infrastructure used by these companies creates potential single points of failure. According to the 2026 Stanford AI Index, NVIDIA accounts for over 60\% of total global AI compute capacity~\cite{aiindex2026}. 
This concentration poses a significant risk of disruption to AI markets in the event of a single failure. 
\newline

Our project is called DAIIM: Decentralized Artificial Intelligence Inference Marketplace.
Our aim is to provide consumers with accurate AI predictions through a decentralized network
of providers, reducing the reliance on centralized solutions.

\subsection{Inputs and Outputs}
\label{subsec:inputs_and_outputs}

Task requesters and inference providers are the primary users of DAIIM (see Section \ref{stakeholders}).

The inputs to the system consist of task definitions, inference requests, network participation, and token stakes.

Task definitions are specifications of a problem to be solved via machine learning, including the 
expected input and output types for a machine learning model. Task definitions may be created 
by any user of the system and serve as a shared contract that defines what task requesters must 
provide and what inference providers must return.

An inference request is an instance of a task definition. 
Inference requests are submitted by task requesters and include all necessary data required for inference providers 
to run their models, in accordance with the associated task definition.

Network participation includes machine learning predictions for individual inference requests that are 
supplied to the network by individual inference providers.

Tokens are input into the system by requesters as payment for the request and providers participating in an inference request as collateral for the prediction accuracy.


The outputs of the system include an aggregated inference result, information about the network, and token payouts.

The system outputs an aggregated inference result related to an inference request to the original task requester, 
based on the predictions from participating providers. 
The aggregated inference result includes the final prediction for a given inference request, as well as information 
about the quality of the prediction.

Information about the network is also maintained in the system and output for transparent viewing for all users, including 
active providers and task definitions.

Tokens are output to users participating in an inference request in relation to the status of that inference request.

\subsection{Stakeholders}
\ref{subsec:stakeholders}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Task requesters} \newline
    Task requesters are people who are requesting inferences to be made for a given task. These people value the decentralized nature of our platform which 
    removes the risk of potential bias and a single point of failure. 
    Some task requesters may value data privacy when making inference requests.
    For example, healthcare researchers would like to make requests to predict various regression inferences (such as biomarker levels) while still complying with privacy constraints. 
    Similarly, decentralized finance platforms would like to make requests to predict various regression inferences (such as stock prices) while still complying with privacy constraints.

    \item \textbf{Inference providers} \newline
    Inference providers are people with computing resources and models who want to monetize their regression models but lack the customer base, infrastructure, and visibility needed to compete with large established AI companies.
    These people usually have specialized or powerful models that can be used to make predictions for a given task.
   
    \item \textbf{Data providers}   \newline
    Data providers are people with data that can be used to train models for a given task.
    They are interested in helping the inference providers improve their models by providing them with data to train on.
    They want to be compensated and credited for their data, 
    but lack a transparent way to share it with model developers and to track how it 
    is used.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Environment}

This software is compatible with any computer running a modern browser that is connected to the internet. There is no hardware component. 

\section{Goals}

This project has five primary goals.

\subsection{Transparency and Decentralization}

Enable inference requesters and model providers to participate in a
transparent inference marketplace that does not rely on a single
centralized entity to manage its core operations. The marketplace's
core logic, including payments, ownership, and settlement, will be
implemented through smart contracts deployed on a public blockchain.
This provides publicly verifiable records of inference requests,
provider submissions, aggregated results, provider reputation updates,
and reward distributions. The system should support participation by
multiple independent providers for each inference request, reducing
reliance on a single model and allowing predictions from diverse sources
to contribute to the aggregated result.

This goal will be evaluated through end-to-end testing. Success will
require at least one inference request to proceed from submission
through provider participation, result aggregation, and settlement.
At least two independent providers must be able to participate in
fulfilling a request. A tester must also be able to verify the relevant
request, submission, aggregation, and settlement records using
on-chain data. The tests must demonstrate that the smart contracts
execute the required marketplace operations and settlement logic
without requiring a centralized authority to approve each transaction.

\subsection{Web3 Usability}

Provide a user-friendly Web3 experience that hides unnecessary
blockchain complexity while maintaining transparency into the actions
and transactions performed by the system. The application should
clearly communicate the purpose and consequences of actions requiring
user authorization, including the transactions and data being signed,
while providing users with direct visibility into the corresponding
on-chain transactions, their status, and relevant state changes.

This goal will be evaluated through usability testing in which
participants complete representative Web3 tasks, such as authorizing
a transaction, checking its status, and locating the corresponding
on-chain record. Participants will also answer questions about the
purpose and consequences of the transactions they authorize.
Success will be measured using task-completion rate, transaction
understanding, and perceived ease of use. The target is for at least
80\% of participants to complete the representative tasks without
assistance, at least 80\% to answer the transaction-understanding
questions correctly, and the average ease-of-use rating to be at
least 4 out of 5.

\subsection{Reliable Inferences}

Enable users to receive accurate and reliable results by aggregating
redundant predictions from multiple model providers rather than
relying on a single prediction. Provider reputation scores will
reflect observed performance and influence the aggregation process,
allowing contributions from providers with stronger records to have
greater influence on the aggregated result. As these scores are
updated over repeated requests, the network can adapt to provider
performance and adjust the influence of their predictions.
Having multiple registered providers should also improve the
availability of inference services by allowing requests to be
fulfilled when some providers are unavailable.

Reliability will be evaluated using the request-completion rate,
defined as the percentage of submitted inference requests that receive
an aggregated result within a predefined time limit. Tests will vary
the number of available and participating providers and include
scenarios in which some providers are unavailable. The completion
rate will be measured under each scenario to assess how provider
availability affects the system's ability to return results. Under
the normal test configuration, the target is for at least 90\% of
submitted requests to receive an aggregated result within the
specified time limit.

Prediction accuracy will be evaluated separately by comparing
aggregated predictions against known ground-truth outcomes using a
metric appropriate to the inference task. For example, a regression
task may be evaluated using mean absolute error, while a
classification task may be evaluated using accuracy or F1 score.
The aggregated predictions will be compared with at least one
baseline, such as predictions from an individual provider or an
unweighted aggregation of provider predictions, to determine whether
the implemented aggregation mechanism improves prediction quality
under the tested conditions. The evaluation will also examine how
provider reputation scores influence the aggregated results.

\subsection{Accessible Inference Experience}

Design an accessible user interface that enables users without
machine-learning experience to submit the inputs required for an
inference task and understand the resulting aggregated prediction
and accompanying information. The interface should use clear
signifiers for available actions, appropriate mappings between task
inputs and controls, constraints that help prevent invalid
submissions, and feedback that communicates request progress and
results.

This goal will be evaluated through usability testing with
participants unfamiliar with machine learning. Participants will
complete representative tasks, including submitting an inference
request, monitoring its progress, and interpreting the resulting
aggregated prediction. Evaluation measures will include task-completion
rate, time on task, user errors, and result comprehension. Success
will require at least 80\% of participants to complete the
representative tasks without assistance and at least 80\% to answer
predefined questions about the aggregated results correctly.
The evaluation will also identify errors that prevent successful
task completion or lead to an incorrect interpretation of the
result. The observed completion times and errors will be recorded
to identify areas for improvement in the interface.

\subsection{Model Provider Innovation}

Encourage independent model providers to participate and improve
their models within the marketplace by providing opportunities to
make their models available to consumers, compete for inference
requests, and receive compensation for their services. The marketplace
should create incentives for providers to improve the quality,
performance, availability, and cost-effectiveness of their models,
as providers that deliver greater value to consumers may be able to
attract more inference requests and earn greater rewards. Improvements
in provider performance may also improve the quality of the network's
aggregated predictions.

This goal will be evaluated through controlled experiments using
simulated providers with different prediction-quality levels and
reputation scores. The experiments will assess whether the reward
and reputation mechanisms respond as intended to differences in
provider performance. In particular, under controlled conditions,
the expected rewards of a provider that improves its prediction
quality will be compared with the rewards it would receive without
that improvement. The experiments will also track provider
reputation scores and aggregated prediction quality across repeated
inference rounds. Aggregated predictions will be evaluated against
known ground-truth outcomes using a task-appropriate metric and
compared with a baseline to determine whether improved provider
predictions lead to improved aggregate prediction quality under
the tested conditions. These experiments will establish whether
the implemented incentive mechanisms reward improved provider
performance and whether such improvements can benefit the network,
without assuming that simulated improvements demonstrate real-world
provider innovation.

\section{Stretch Goals}

This project has two stretch goals, which outline additional
features that could be implemented after the primary goals have been
achieved.

\subsection{Expanded AI Model and Inference Support}

Extend the marketplace beyond regression to support additional categories of AI inference and more complex tasks. This is considered a stretch
goal because supporting multiple model architectures and more complex
inference workflows would require additional development beyond the core
marketplace functionality established by the primary goals.

\subsection{Private AI Inference}

Enable inference on private or sensitive data while protecting that data
from unauthorized access during execution through the use of Trusted
Execution Environments (TEEs). This is considered a stretch goal because
incorporating TEEs would introduce additional security, infrastructure,
and integration requirements beyond those necessary to support the core
marketplace and inference functionality.

\subsection{Data Provider Participation}
Enable data providers to contribute training data to the marketplace and receive 
compensation when that data is used by inference providers to train or improve their 
models. Data contributions and their use would be recorded on-chain, allowing data 
providers to be credited and rewarded in a transparent manner. This is considered a 
stretch goal because it would require mechanisms for data submission, storage, access 
control, and attribution of model improvements to specific datasets, all of which 
extend beyond the core inference marketplace established by the primary goals.


\section{Custom Documents (CDs)}

\subsection{Cognitive Walkthrough}

A cognitive walkthrough will be performed using a wireframe prototype of the 
planned interface of the application from the point of view of the inference requesters. 
This will aid in mapping the entire process of \progname{} and generally smooth out any remaining 
ambiguity. The results of the cognitive walkthrough will provide essential insight into the 
required user feedback and how to make the UI accessible and learnable for the expected users 
to ensure the application is simple to understand and enjoyable to use. 

\subsection{Business Plan}

A business plan will be developed once a proof of concept has met expectations. The goal 
of the business plan is to detail how the decentralized AI marketplace can be implemented 
such that developers and stakeholders can be compensated, and will further explain the 
business strategy behind the tokenomics. The business plan would include a description
of the problem and the market need, the market size and business model, the cost breakdown,
the competition and positioning, and the execution plan and milestones.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{What went well while writing this deliverable?}

    Our team was incredibly agreeable and had no trouble communicating our ideas for the project or our concerns about the scope. The team worked smoothly to accomplish the requirements for the deliverable. Everyone cooperated with one another, and it was easy to split up the workload evenly without disagreements or conflict. We were able to easily schedule multiple group meetings, which facilitated group discussions that gave everyone a deeper understanding of the project.

    \item \textbf{What pain points did you experience during this deliverable, and how did you resolve them?}

    There were varying levels of experience in the subject matter of ML, blockchains, inferences, and decentralized AI systems. Members who were less familiar with the content did some research on the subject matter, and members who were more experienced were extremely cooperative in answering questions in a non-judgmental and non-condescending manner. Notes of previous research performed by Robert were also shared as supplementary resources. Our team organized a couple of meetings to discuss the content in this document to ensure everyone was comfortable with the material and to encourage necessary discussion to determine the problem, users, goals, and scope of the project.

    \item \textbf{How did you and your team adjust the scope of your goals to ensure they are suitable for a Capstone project (not overly ambitious but also of appropriate complexity for a senior design project)?}

    Our team discussed our skills and experience, and ideas for features of the application. We considered the timeline and budget, and ultimately we think we can reasonably accomplish the project over the next few terms given our skill and experience levels.

    Initially, we brainstormed goals that we later deemed to be too ambitious for the scope of the project. For example, we had planned to try to implement multiple types of inferences. Upon further inspection, we realized that not only was this goal too solution-oriented, but it was also beyond what we could determine was reasonably feasible within the timeline of the project, considering the other features we are planning to implement. We instead included this as a stretch goal.
\end{enumerate}
